# Background: the owner's design views

SKILL.md is the current guidance. This file is background for when you want the reasoning behind it or an example. Examples from specific games are there to illustrate, not as rules. Project-specific guidance lives in each project.

## Routes produce different games
- **Mechanical, bottom-up:** a designer chasing a tight maths tension ends up with tight play and disjointed theme, unless they make a later top-down pass.
- **Flavor, top-down:** a designer starting from a fantasy evokes it well, but the mechanics come out forced and over-designed.
  - *Example, from one project:* migratory birds that nest, lay eggs and leave. It evoked birds, but it was clunky, too complex, and a poor fit for a game with tight mechanical bounds.
- **Iteration:** it can rescue a poor start. A non-obvious fit can end up more artistic than the obvious one. Letting a doubtful idea keep iterating is part of the craft.
- **When to switch routes:** when a route isn't working, or when the design has a gap to fill. Otherwise it's down to taste and the goal.
- **The owner's own default:** top-down mechanical. Look at what the thing needs and how its effects should be distributed, sketch many loose concepts, then work out what each one needs.

## Four example routes to the same genre
*Example: resource trading.*
- **Top-down mechanical:** list the effects the game needs (generate, convert, buy, sell, restrict trades), then find pieces for each.
- **Top-down flavor:** start from a theme concept and let the pieces follow from it.
- **Bottom-up flavor:** "I like beer. How would hops, wheat and yeast work?"
- **Bottom-up mechanical:** start from one puzzle. A conversion gives a better rate of return but not what you need now: efficiency against what you can cash in. The supporting rules (an inventory limit, time pressure) are chosen to protect that tension.

## New content in a mature game
- **A whole faction:** often top-down. *Example:* sacrifice as a theme, so discarding as a mechanic. Discarding was too costly for the genre, so the faction got many pieces that grow stronger when discarded.
- **One piece:** often designed from its role. "I need a late, big payoff for a working setup" gives the mechanic, then the name and the theme.
- **Surgical:** map the set's mix of roles and where each arrives in the progression, then fill the gap. Top-down alone risks the wrong mix, such as too many economy pieces and too few payoffs.

## Systems, mechanics, content
- **System:** a rule of the game, made of a concept plus particulars. In a mature game you tune the particulars and rarely change the concept. Its job is to set the right incentives.
- **Content:** much can be done with mechanics and content alone.
- **Where to fix:** pick the level whose fix costs least everywhere else.
  - *Example:* in a game with no timer, any repeatable gain loop is infinite: a player can leave an auto-clicker running.
  - Limiting each piece makes text long, puts more concepts on the player, and makes content less fun.
  - One rule that bounds the loop, such as rising costs for repeating an action, frees the content to be generous.
- **Spotting a system problem:** you keep seeing the same issue, whatever you're designing. Telling content from mechanic from system problems is intuition, and agents struggle with it. Persistent, uncertain issues are where the owner steps in.

## Players
- **Player profiles:** know them for the game. Some players want to win with whatever works. Others hold strong strategic preferences and will lose rather than play what they dislike.
- **Audiences:** serve the primary audience, and give secondary ones something without hurting the primary.
- **The one model we use:** MTG's player profiles, in their extended form.
  - **Motivation:**
    - Timmy wants big, exciting moments.
    - Johnny wants to express himself and find clever combinations.
    - Spike wants to win.
  - **Aesthetics:**
    - Vorthos loves flavor and story.
    - Melvin loves mechanical elegance.
  - **Link to routes:** flavor-first design tends to serve Vorthos, and mechanical-first design tends to serve Melvin. Check that a design isn't serving only one of them.
- **Johnny needs:** pieces that interweave, overlap and get used outside their intended purpose. Designing a faction strictly top-down for itself gives pieces that only work inside it.

## Judgment over rules
- There are no hard rules.
- **Obvious route:** if one is clearly right, take it.
- **Uncertain call:** try several options and compare.
- **Fun before balance:** give power generously, then dial it back.
- **Reactions:** the owner's reaction decides what gets iterated, not what gets decided.
- **Evaluation criteria:** keep them few. Too many make evaluation impossible.

## Evidence behind the route table
Small samples: one run per route in each test.
- **Test 1, one new card for an existing faction:**
  - **Top-down mechanical:** gave the best card. It found an empty category of effect and filled it with a piece that bridges two strategies.
  - **Flavor routes:** gave the most creative card and the plainest.
  - **Surgical:** found a real gap but placed the card's power badly.
- **Test 2, a full 24-piece faction:**
  - **Bottom-up mechanical:** the owner's favourite. Distinctive and ambitious, and it took about 3× longer.
  - **Surgical:** best power for its place, and the most ready to test.
  - **Top-down mechanical:** better balanced than the flavor routes, but less interesting.
  - **Flavor routes:** the most creative, with the widest power swings.
  - **Lore anchoring:** three of five designers converged on the theme the brief's lore led with. The two that escaped started from the game, not the theme.
- **Every route:** missed power-for-place checks and made rules mistakes.
