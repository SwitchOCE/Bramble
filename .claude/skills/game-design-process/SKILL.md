# Game design process

This skill is about how to design, not about any one game. There are no hard rules in design. These are ways of thinking to reach for when they help, not a procedure: use judgment, and don't walk through every section by default.

**Project specifics live in the project.** Before you design, read the project's own design guidance: its rules, player profiles, power references, known failure patterns and past decisions. If that guidance conflicts with this skill on a game-specific matter, the project wins.

## Which level is the problem at?
1. **System rule:** what the game is, such as how resources arrive or how you lose. In a mature game this rarely changes.
2. **Parameters:** that rule's numbers and particulars. Tune these freely.
3. **Mechanic:** a reusable rule that many pieces share, such as a keyword or a faction's mechanic.
4. **Content:** the individual pieces, levels or encounters. These may break the rules above.

If the project keeps a list of possible causes (a piece, its role, how it was measured, and so on), use it to find the cause. Then use this ladder to choose where to fix it.
- **Recurring problems:** when the same problem keeps coming back across unrelated pieces, suspect a higher level.
- **Before adding a cap:** before you add a cap, limiter or exception to a piece, count how many already exist.
- **Not yours to change:** if the higher level isn't yours to change, flag it upward instead of patching. When unsure, sketch one fix per level and compare them.
- **Endless loops:** a loop that players can repeat without limit is often cheaper to bound with one rule than with text on every piece. Content safeguards are fine too. Growth that compounds over a game is often what you want, so don't flatten it.

## Checks
These are for designers before handing over, and for critics and reviewers.

Check these first:
- **Power for its place,** where players acquire pieces: name the two existing pieces you compared it against that arrive at the same point. Say when the player gets it, what they'll have by then, and what they'd take instead. Use the project's power references.
- **Play pattern:** what does the player actually do from moment to moment? Is each step a decision or a chore?
- **The path through a set:** walk it in the order a player meets it. Would they take each early piece on its own merits, and does it make them want the next one? Look for stages a good player would skip.

Then:
- **Rules:** check against the project's rulebook.
- **Players:** use MTG's profiles: Timmy, Johnny and Spike, plus Vorthos (flavor) and Melvin (mechanics). Serve the project's primary profile without abandoning the others. Where combination play matters, ask what each piece does outside its intended home. Make sure flavor and mechanics don't serve only Vorthos or only Melvin.
- **Wording,** where pieces carry text: it fits, and every term is defined.
- **Generosity:** fun comes before balance. Be generous first, then dial back.

A design you check yourself tends to pass. When the stakes are high, have someone else run these checks.

## Routes
Where a design starts shapes where it ends up. Different routes lead to different games, not just different paths to the same one.
- **Altitude:** top-down starts from a system or concept and fills in the parts. Bottom-up starts from one concrete piece and lets the structure emerge.
- **Origin:** mechanical-first starts from the logic, the maths or a player tension. Flavor-first starts from the world, the theme, or what things represent.
- **Surgical:** start from the existing whole. Find the gap, then place a piece by its role and by where in the player's progression it arrives.

Say in one line which route you're taking and why. Left alone, agents tend to default to top-down, flavor-first; if that's your route, check that you actually chose it. If a route stalls, switch.

These tendencies come from one run per route. They aren't laws.

| Route | Good at | Watch for |
|---|---|---|
| Top-down mechanical | Sound structure, role players, balance | Safe and dull; overcooking |
| Bottom-up mechanical | Distinctive core mechanics, real tension | Theme that feels bolted on |
| Top-down flavor | Wide, bold idea lists | Wild power levels; forced, over-designed mechanics |
| Bottom-up flavor | Mechanics that pair well, strong theme | Loose balance |
| Surgical | Pieces sized for their place; ready to test | Less creative. The gap tells you the role, not the power |

## Useful ways to think, by job
- **Blank-slate ideas:** the flavor routes or bottom-up mechanical. Keep the wild results in an idea library rather than cutting them. A poor fit can become the most interesting piece after iteration.
- **A core mechanic for something new:** bottom-up mechanical or surgical. In testing these escaped anchoring by theme, and bottom-up mechanical made the owner's favourite set. If the decision is high-stakes and uncertain, take two or three options to the owner.
- **Building a set of related pieces:** whichever route found the ideas, top-down mechanical is the owner's preferred way to structure the set. Decide which kinds of effect it needs (sources, enablers, payoffs, support, bridges to other sets), how many of each, and where they arrive. Then fill the gaps from the library.
- **One new piece in an existing game:** start from what the whole is missing. The owner prefers surgical. In one test, top-down mechanical made the best piece by finding a category of effect with almost nothing in it. Either way, design from the role and name it last.
- **Stuck, samey or bland:** switch route, or rework ideas from the library into role players.

## Briefing other designers
- **Theme:** a theme given up front anchors everyone to the same idea. For the mechanical routes, give the theme after the mechanic is set.
- **Examples:** designers copy them. Give none, or give everyone the same one.
- **Parallel designers:** give each one a different route. Only the person comparing their results can see sameness; a single designer can't.
