## Part A: the seed — The Changelings

**Who they are.** The Fair Folk under the hills of Mosswood, the Atlantic oak rainforest of standing stones and holy wells. No changeling has a face of its own. Each one arrives in someone else's place: a cradle, a supper table, a shop counter, a regiment's front rank. It wears the borrowed shape well, never perfectly. The whiskers are a hair too long, the paws are left-handed, the shadow is a moment late, the voice is right but the old jokes are gone. By the time anyone notices, the swap is weeks old.

**The folklore.** From Ireland to Germany, tales tell of fairies taking a baby or young mother and leaving one of their own instead. The changeling is wizened, always hungry, and never grows. In the best-known test the mother brews in eggshells and the startled changeling gives itself away: in the Welsh telling, "I have seen the acorn before the oak, but never brewing in an eggshell". Sometimes the fairies leave a **stock** instead: a log carved and glamoured to look like the one taken, which lies in the bed, sickens and seems to die. Cold iron, open scissors over the cradle and rowan kept them off. A smear of fairy ointment on one eye lets a midwife see the fair folk as they are. One night under the hill can be a hundred years outside.

**Natural history they admire.** The large blue butterfly's caterpillar smells and sounds like an ant grub. Red ants carry it into their nest, where it spends the winter eating their young. Some *Volucella* hoverflies look like wasps or bumblebees and lay their eggs inside those very nests. The bee orchid looks like a bee yet in Britain nearly always pollinates itself: a disguise that outlived its use.

**Society.** A parody of a very proper placement bureau, and of the swapped-at-birth melodrama. The **Board of Exchanges** sits in the hill and insists that every swap is fair, because something was always left in return. Rank goes by how long your last swap went unnoticed. Being unmasked is only an embarrassment: you come home to polite applause. Changelings are fond of the folk they replace and often do their jobs better.

**Notable figures.**
- **Bettie Underhill**, Registrar of Exchanges, whose ledger lists everything ever left and nothing ever taken.
- **Grandfer Acorn**, so old he remembers the great oak as an acorn. Unmasked by eggshells three times; still can't help remarking.
- **Wicker Moll**, Mosswood's finest carver of stocks. Hers sigh in their sleep.
- **Tam Twice**, who has been every member of one badger family at least once, and two of them at the same Michaelmas dinner.
- **Penny Second**, who stood in for a stoat duchess for a season. The household turned the real duchess away as the impostor.
- **Pip Unlike**, an apprentice whose copies always come out mirror-backwards.
- **The Lady Under the Hill**, whom nobody has seen as herself. She may be any of them.

**Places.** The **Knowe**, a green hill with seven doors. The **Exchange Room**, lined with empty cradles and coat-pegs. **Hob's Ring**, a ring of fairy-ring mushrooms that widens outward year by year as the fungus spreads. The **Lost Hour**, a hollow where clocks stop.

**Objects and icons.** Eggshells (their undoing), open scissors, horseshoes and rowan sprigs (things they dread), carved stocks of alder, fairy ointment, glamour-veils, fairy gold that turns to leaves at dawn, the ledger, borrowed coats worn inside out.

**Themes.** Becoming what you replace. The copy that is almost right. The swap noticed too late. A bargain that looks fair. The impostor who does the job better. Who is the real one?

**The play it suggests:** Swapped-in fae who become what they replace: copies of the shop's best, of your own carry, or of whatever they are traded for.
