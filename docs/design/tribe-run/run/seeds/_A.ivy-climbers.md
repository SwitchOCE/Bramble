## Part A: the seed — The Ivy and Mistletoe

**Who they are.** The climbing plants of hedges and old trees: ivy, mistletoe, honeysuckle and dodder, with wild clematis and the hop as country cousins. None of them stands alone. They live in Hedgemoot's hedges and orchards and up Mosswood's ancient oaks, always on something taller. The woodland calls them social climbers, and they take it as a compliment: they attach themselves to whoever matters, rise with them, and take credit for the view.

**The real plants.** Ivy holds on with thousands of tiny rootlets that glue themselves to bark and stone; it takes only support from its host and seldom harms a healthy tree. Young ivy has lobed leaves. Once it climbs into the light it turns adult, with plain oval leaves, autumn flowers (a late feast for wasps, hoverflies and the ivy bee) and black berries in late winter, when birds have little else. Mistletoe is a half-parasite: its green leaves make its food, but it taps its host's branch for water and minerals. It grows in round clumps on apple, lime, poplar and hawthorn, and only rarely on oak. Birds plant it: the mistle thrush eats the sticky white berries and guards a good clump against other birds all winter, and the blackcap wipes seeds off its beak onto a branch. Honeysuckle twines clockwise round hazel tightly enough to carve barley-sugar spirals into it, and smells strongest at night, for moths. Dodder has no leaves and, once it has caught a host, no roots: a seedling has only days to find a plant, grows toward its scent, wraps it in orange threads and drinks from it.

**Society.** A parody of Regency high society: the Season, patrons, hangers-on and the social register. Breeding is everything, and the best is oak mistletoe, so rare its families have never touched the ground. Ivy is the climbing middle class, quietly up every important trunk. Honeysuckle are flatterers and poets in need of a patron; dodder are gatecrashers everyone ends up feeding. The Season peaks at midwinter with the kissing-bough balls: a berry is picked for each kiss, and when the berries run out, so does the kissing. At any party the question is not who you are but whom you are on.

**Notable figures.** **Lady Hedera Overlook**, ivy dowager, who has climbed every notable oak in the shire and keeps a guest-book of her hosts. **The Honourable Viscum Highbough**, oak mistletoe of insufferably old blood, who quotes Pliny about himself. **Woodbine**, a honeysuckle poet who dedicates every sonnet to whoever holds him up. **Cousin Dodder**, rootless, leafless, charming, and already wrapped round your arm. **Old Man's Beard**, a wild clematis hermit, feathery-whiskered in winter, who claims he once knew the druids. **Missel**, a mistle thrush footman who chases every other bird off his lady's berries.

**Places.** Hedgemoot's old orchards, where mistletoe hangs in the apple trees and the midwinter mistletoe fair is held. **The Grand Oak** of Mosswood, the woodland's most fashionable address. Ivy-clad ruins, held up (they say) by the ivy. Hazel coppice, the honeysuckle's salons. Heather commons, where dodder lurks.

**Objects and icons.** The golden sickle and white robes of Pliny's druids, who cut oak mistletoe on the sixth day of the moon; the kissing bough; the ivy bush, the old tavern sign; trellises, ladders and visiting cards; barley-sugar walking sticks; the guest-book; the ivy's emblem motto, *I die where I attach myself*.

**Themes.** Who you know over who you are; borrowed height; climbing to the light and blooming there; the friend and the parasite, alike from a distance; clinging loyalty; green in winter; mistletoe's old scandal as the one plant that never swore Baldr's oath.

**The play it suggests:** climbers that don't stand alone grow on another unit and rise with their host, so choosing whom to climb is the game (a suggestion only).
