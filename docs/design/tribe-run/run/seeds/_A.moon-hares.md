## Part A: the seed — The Moon Hares

**Who they are.** Brown hares of **Chalkdown**, ancient downland of rolling turf, beech clumps, dew ponds, barrows, hill-forts and giant white figures cut in the chalk. Its shepherd-druids recut those figures each generation so the gods remember them; the Moon Hares keep the night side of that work. Not soldiers but moon-witches: a long-legged sisterhood who read the sky, change shape by moonlight and settle the year's business in the boxing ring.

**The real animal.** Brown hares do not burrow. Each lies up by day in a **form**, a shallow scrape in grass or plough, trusting stillness, then bolts at the last moment: one of Europe's fastest runners, zigzagging and doubling back to throw off pursuit. They feed mostly at night. Leverets are born furred and open-eyed, and lie alone all day; the mother visits briefly around dusk to nurse them. The famous **March boxing** is mostly a female fending off a male who pushes his suit too soon: she rears up and beats him off with her forepaws. A doe can even conceive a second litter while still carrying the first.

**Folklore.** In British and European lore a hare running too fast at dusk might be a witch in borrowed shape. The Scottish witch Isobel Gowdie confessed in 1662 to a charm beginning "I shall go into a hare, with sorrow and sigh and meikle care", and in country lore a witch-hare could be harmed only by silver. Caesar wrote that the Britons would not eat hare; Boudica is said to have loosed one from her cloak before battle to read the omens by its run. Lewis Carroll's March Hare grew from the old saying "mad as a March hare". The **hare in the moon** is seen in much of the world. Church roofs in Devon and Paderborn Cathedral carry the **three hares**: three hares chasing in a circle that share three ears between them.

**Society.** The coven is run like a gentlemen's boxing club that is also an almanac society. Every quarrel, inheritance and courtship is settled by a bout in the ring, under rules older than the barrows, refereed by a senior witch who reads the result as prophecy. Does (the **jills**) hold the rings and the almanac; bucks (the **jacks**) come courting each spring and get boxed back across the field, as is proper. They speak in lunar dates, keep strict hours and grow alarmingly giddy as the moon fills.

**Notable figures.** **Mother Meikle Care**, eldest of the coven, who knows the long charm by heart and the short one by accident. **Jill Longshanks**, undefeated in the spring ring and bored. **Old Wat**, a grizzled jack thrown out of every ring for longer than anyone can remember, still proposing. **Dame Waxwell**, keeper of the almanac, who has never once been surprised by the sky. **Sister Silverpenny**, who walks among the shepherds by day in another shape.

**Places.** **The Moon-Scour**, a white hare cut into the chalk, recut each generation. **The Ring**, a fairy ring by the dew pond where the bouts are fought. **The Forms**, a hillside of scrapes where leverets wait out the day. **The Old Rampart**, a hill-fort where the coven meets at the equinox.

**Objects and icons.** Boxing ribbons and bandaged forepaws, the almanac with its phase wheels, silver sixpences, hare's-foot charms, the three-hares disc, dew-pond mirrors for scrying, crescent moons stitched on every sleeve, tufts of fur on the turf after a bout.

**Themes.** Phase and change, waxing and waning, the full moon as a giddy turning point, ritual as sport, sisters and suitors, stillness then sudden speed, children left alone and trusted, borrowed shapes, omens read from a run.

**The play it suggests:** the tribe changes with a moon that waxes and wanes across rounds, so you build for the phase that is coming, and the full moon transforms the team.
