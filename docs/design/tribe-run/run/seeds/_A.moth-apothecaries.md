## Part A: the seed — The Moth Apothecaries

**Who they are.** A guild of night-flying chemists, herbalists and fortune-tellers who keep a lamplit dispensary on the heath edge of the Moor & Bog, where the heather meets the night meadows. They open at dusk and shut at first light. Customers come for remedies and leave with an omen. They are moth folk of every kind, from fat hawkmoths to dusty noctuids and bright burnets.

**Real natural history.** Moths circle lamps and candles. Most likely they keep their backs to the brightest light, which was once always the sky, so a lamp makes them spiral. Their wings are covered in loose scales that rub off as "dust" (Lepidoptera means "scale wings"). The **peppered moth** is the textbook case of evolution: pale and speckled to match lichen, it turned mostly black in Britain's sooty industrial towns during the 1800s and went pale again once the air was cleaned. The **emperor moth** wears four eyespots, and the **eyed hawk-moth** flashes a hidden pair; both startle birds. The **death's-head hawkmoth**, a migrant from the south with a skull pattern on its back, squeaks when handled and slips into beehives to drink honey, passing because it smells like the hive. **Six-spot burnets** are red and black because they carry cyanide compounds. Many moths hear bats coming and dive. Adult emperor moths have no working mouths and live a few days on what they ate as caterpillars. Male emperors track a female's scent across kilometres of moor with feathered antennae. Moths pollinate honeysuckle and evening primrose by night. Real British moths have names like the Alchymist, the Herald, the Uncertain, the Spectacle, the Old Lady and the Mother Shipton.

**Society and what it parodies.** Part London's Worshipful Society of Apothecaries (chartered 1617, keepers of the Chelsea Physic Garden), part Victorian chemist's shop, part almanac astrologer. Recipes are handed down by mouth in the dark, and each apprentice adds a line to the master's. Rank goes by how close you work to the lamp. Seniors are scorched at the edges and proud of it. Their motto: "Nearer the Flame." Their enemies are the bats and nightjars that hunt the meadows, the skep-beekeepers of the moor, whose hives they rob, and the moth-ball, which no apothecary will name aloud.

**Notable figures.** **Dame Pavonia**, Mistress of the Guild, an emperor moth who has never eaten and never will. **Atropos**, the death's-head hawkmoth: honey-thief and night dispenser, she squeaks when cornered. **Old Carbonaria**, a peppered moth who went black in the soot years and keeps the coal-dark ledger. **Mother Shipton**, the omen-reader, whose wings bear a witch's face. **The Herald**, who winters in cellars and announces each season. **The Alchymist**, a rare visitor said to hold the first recipe. **The Uncertain**, an apprentice who is never sure of a dose. **Burnet**, red and black, the keeper of the poison cabinet.

**Places.** The Lamplit Dispensary with its show globes, the coloured glass jars that glow in the window. The Physic Meadow of honeysuckle, evening primrose and night-scented stock. The Sugared Oaks, whose bark is painted with treacle and rum. Skep Row, the hives on the heather. The Burnt Ring around the great lamp. The Cedar Press, where nothing of theirs survives.

**Objects and icons.** Phials, mortar and pestle, show globes, wing-dust in paper twists, treacle pots, candle snuffers, almanacs of bad news, scent letters, silk cocoons, a skull on velvet.

**Themes.** Drawn to the flame in full knowledge of the danger. Recipes that build on one another. Omens nobody asked for. Moths as souls of the dead (Greek *psyche* means soul and butterfly). Changing colour to fit the times. Bluffing with false eyes. Short bright adult lives. Theft by disguise.

**The play it suggests:** Night apothecaries who brew by lamplight: the tribe grows by casting spells, and its spells get stronger the more of them it casts.
