## Part A: the seed — The Cuckoo Clockworks

**Who they are.** A clockmakers' guild of cuckoos and dormice in **Beechhold**, the woodland's Black Forest: grey beech trunks over copper leaf litter, misty fir ridges, giant-roofed farmhouses, clear tarns and charcoal smoke. Cuckoos are the masters, precision craftsmen who keep every hour to the second. Edible dormice are the apprentices, who wind, oil and carve and doze at the bench. The whole town runs on carved mechanical time, and dormice hibernate by law. The joke they never see: punctuality fanatics who are also brood parasites, smuggling their eggs into other people's nests.

**The real animals.** The common cuckoo raises no young of its own. A female watches a host pair build, slips in while they are away, removes one of their eggs and lays her own in about ten seconds. Each female line specialises in one host, such as reed warblers, meadow pipits or dunnocks, and its eggs mimic that host's. The egg hatches early, having begun developing inside her, and within a day or two the blind, naked chick heaves the host's eggs over the rim one by one, balanced in a hollow on its back. Its begging sounds like a whole brood, so the foster parents work as if for many, feeding a red-mouthed chick several times their own size. Females are barred like a sparrowhawk, which scares hosts off the nest. Only males call "cuck-oo"; females give a bubbling chuckle. Adults eat the hairy caterpillars other birds refuse and leave for Africa in high summer; the young follow weeks later, alone, never having met a parent. Edible dormice (German *Siebenschläfer*, "seven-sleeper") hibernate seven months or more, skip breeding in years when the beeches drop no mast, were fattened in jars by the Romans, and move into birds' nest boxes, sometimes eating the eggs.

**Society.** A parody of the Black Forest clock trade and the old craft guild: masters, journeymen on their wander years and apprentices, all bound by bylaws, seals and timetables. Respectability is everything and lateness the one true sin. Peddlers carry clocks on their backs from farm to farm; all know what else a cuckoo leaves behind, and nobody says it. Guild families are named for the host they "place with", and each family's egg-painting pattern book is its most guarded secret. Folklore follows them: the first cuckoo heralds spring, you turn the coins in your pocket when you hear it, and children count its calls to learn how many years they have left.

**Notable figures.** **Grand Horologe Ottilie Gugger**, guildmistress, who has never once been early. **Kasimir the Peddler**, who sells a clock at every farmhouse and leaves something in its nest box. **Frau Wechselbalg** (*"changeling"*), keeper of the pattern books. **Old Unruh**, a dormouse apprentice asleep since autumn and still marked present. **Little Gernegroß** (*"would-be-big"*), a chick three times his foster mother's size, demanding supper. **Mother Rohrsang**, the reed warbler who feeds him and insists he takes after her side.

**Places.** **Tickenau**, a clock-town of railway-house gables on a fir ridge; the **Tower of the Hour**, whose little doors all open at once; **Bellows Hall**, where calls are tuned; the **Sleeping Vault**, where dormice hibernate in rows of jars; the reedbeds and pipit moors where the eggs go out each May.

**Objects and icons.** Pine-cone weights on chains, bellows and two pipes a third apart, carved oak-leaf clock fronts, the little door that snaps open on the hour, pocket watches and timetables, painted mimic eggs, wind-up keys, nest boxes, sleeping jars, the pompom hat.

**Themes.** Punctuality, the hour striking, mimicry and changelings, borrowed hospitality, fosterage, the cuckoo in the nest, sleep against clockwork, the herald of spring, parents gone before the children grow up.

**The play it suggests:** brood parasites who slip their eggs into the opponent's line-up, where they hatch on a timer mid-fight and turn on their hosts.
