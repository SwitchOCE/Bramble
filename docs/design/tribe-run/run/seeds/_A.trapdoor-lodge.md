## Part A: the seed — The Trapdoor Lodge

**Who they are.** A secret fraternal lodge of spiders on the sunny hedge banks of the Hedgemoot and the heaths of the Chalkdown. They are not insects, as any member will tell you at length. Purse-web spiders and trapdoor spiders make up the senior degrees; wolf spiders, garden spiders and money spiders are lodge members of lesser rank. They are polite and patient to the point of rudeness: they let you finish your sentence, your meal and your whole campaign before they answer.

**The real spiders.** Britain's only tarantula relative, the **purse-web spider** (*Atypus affinis*), lives on heaths and banks in a silk tube that runs down into the soil. Its top lies along the surface, dressed with grit. When an insect walks across it, the spider stabs up through the silk with its big fangs, pulls the prey inside and mends the slit. A female may live for years in the same tube. Autumn males wander, and tap on a female's tube before going in; her young winter in it and leave in spring. The Mediterranean's **trapdoor spiders** (*Cteniza*, *Nemesia*) close their burrows with a hinged lid of silk and soil. Lids are thick "cork" doors or thin "wafers". The spider holds the lid shut with its fangs and claws, or waits under it at night with its legs at the rim. **Wolf spiders** (*Pardosa*) hunt the heath by sight instead of with webs. The mother carries her egg sac on her spinnerets, then the hatchlings on her back. The **garden spider**, with a white cross on its back, often eats its old web and spins a new one, then waits in its retreat with a foot on a signal thread. On autumn mornings ballooning **money spiders** leave whole fields under gossamer.

**The Lodge.** A gentle parody of the old fraternal orders and gentlemen's clubs: degrees and passwords, aprons and regalia, minutes read aloud, a ballot box where one black ball keeps you out, and a doorkeeper (the **Tyler**) who guards the door. Every door has its knock; a wrong knock gets you nothing. Meetings start when the last member arrives, and that is always one of the seniors, on purpose. The common folk say "let a spider run alive" and think it lucky when a money spider lands on them; the Lodge does nothing to stop the rumour that it arranges both.

**Notable figures.** **Grand Tyler Mabyn Underlid**, a purse-web matriarch who hasn't left her tube in seven summers and chairs every meeting from it. **Signora Corkhinge**, visiting from the southern lodges, whose lid nobody has ever seen open. **Brother Pardo Ashby**, the wolf-spider Steward, who brings forty nieces to every meeting on his back. **Old Diadem**, the garden-spider Recorder, who writes the minutes in a fresh web each dawn and eats yesterday's. **Tapwell**, a young wandering male who has knocked on every door on the bank and been let in at none. **Gossamer Jill**, a money spider who carries messages between lodges on the wind.

**Places.** The Bank, a south-facing sandy slope where the lids are; Hawthorn Bank Lodge, under a hedge older than the parish; the Under-Chamber, which may not exist; the Dew Gallery, orb webs strung across the gorse at dawn.

**Objects and icons.** Hinged lids, grit-dressed tubes, knock codes, silk aprons, the ballot box of beetle-shell balls, the Rule Book, signal threads, dew beads, egg sacs, the white cross, gossamer.

**Themes.** Patience as virtue and insult; secrecy; the door that looks like ground; being underestimated; who gets let in and who gets blackballed; mothers who carry the family; the long wait and the sudden strike.

**The play it suggests:** a suggestion only, spiders who wait off the board under their trapdoors and spring into the fight when the moment comes, so the board you show is not the board you fight with.
