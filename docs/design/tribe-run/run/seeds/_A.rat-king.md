## Part A: the seed — The Rat King

**Who they are.** Not a horde but a single knotted hive-mind: dozens of rats whose tails have tangled into one gossiping body that says "we", votes on everything and can't agree which way to walk. They live in the towns of **Beechhold**, Central European beech-and-fir country of grey cathedral trunks, copper leaf litter, misty fir ridges and giant-roofed farmhouses, in the warm spaces under the town. A committee that is literally tied together.

**The folklore and the real animal.** The *Rattenkönig* is German folklore with real specimens behind it: rats found with their tails knotted fast, reported mostly from Germany and northern Europe since the sixteenth century. The largest preserved one, 32 rats, was found in a miller's fireplace at Buchheim in 1828 and is kept in the Mauritianum museum in Altenburg; an Estonian farmer found another in 2005, now in Tartu. Most were black rats, whose tails are long and supple, and most were found in winter. The usual explanation is a cramped nest where huddling rats' tails get glued together by sap, dirt or frost. Older lore pictured a king rat enthroned on its subjects' knotted tails and fed by them, and the knots were read as bad omens. In German, *Rattenkönig* still means a hopeless tangle of problems. Real rats are very social: they groom each other, they smell what a nestmate ate on its breath and then choose that food themselves, and they make ultrasonic chirps when tickled, which researchers call laughter. They free trapped cagemates and help rats that helped them. They distrust anything new, tasting a crumb first, and run the same routes along walls.

**Society and what it parodies.** In German, *Rat* means council (*Rathaus*, *Stadtrat*), and these folk are the small-town council taken literally: motions, seconds, minutes, points of order, a quorum that can never leave the room. The pub in the town hall cellar is the *Ratskeller*, and they consider it theirs. Gossip is how the knot thinks. News runs tail to tail and arrives amended twice. Every decision is unanimous, eventually.

**Notable figures.** **Speaker Brotkrume**, at the very centre, who calls every vote and has never once had the floor. **Ilse the Minute-Taker**, who writes everything down, including what the knot was about to say. **Little Fips, the Outermost**, the only rat who can see where they're going; motion to listen to him, defeated. **Old Gundel**, who breaks deadlocks by tickling until everyone laughs into agreement. **Taster Wendelin**, who nibbles everything new first; the knot sniffs his breath and then decides on supper. **The Thirty-Two of Altenburg**, the founding assembly, under glass. Hanging over all of them is **the Piper** of Hamelin. The legend dates the lost children to 1284 (the rats were added later): a piper in motley led the rats out and, unpaid by the burghers, led the children away. In Hamelin's *Bungelosenstraße*, no music is played to this day. His tune is the only thing that ever made a rat king walk in one direction.

**Places.** The Ratskeller, the mill chimney, the granary loft, the council chamber under the floorboards, the runways along the walls, the cold shed where a new knot tangles.

**Objects and icons.** The knot itself, a wheel of heads turned outward; the gavel; the minute book; voting beans; the town-hall bell; cheese rinds; a tickling feather; the Piper's pipe and motley.

**Themes.** We before I, consensus and its cost, procedure as comedy, gossip as a nervous system, entanglement you can't undo, warmth in a cold winter, what one feels all feel, loyalty to the stuck, fear of the one tune that leads you all away.

**The play it suggests:** bodies tied together into one knot, where what happens to one spreads along the others, so you build the knot, not the unit.
