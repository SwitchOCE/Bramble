## Part A: the seed — The Will-o'-the-Wisps

**Who they are.** Small fae lights of the woodland's wet places: the fens and carrs of Lowdike, where the drains and dykes run, and the moors and domed bogs of Moor & Bog, with their sphagnum, bog cotton, low fog and clapper bridges. At night they hang above the reeds, always a few steps ahead of anyone following. Every local name is a different face of them: will-o'-the-wisp ("Will with a wisp", a wisp being a twist of burning straw), jack-o'-lantern, hobby lantern, spunkie, Joan the Wad, the German *Irrlicht* and the French *feu follet*. Scholars call them *ignis fatuus*, "foolish fire".

**The real lights.** Marsh lights are a real report, never fully explained. Rotting plants under bog water give off methane, the "marsh gas" Alessandro Volta collected from the mud of Lake Maggiore in 1776. The usual guess is that traces of phosphine catch fire in air and light it. Other true glows of the woodland belong in their company: foxfire, the cold light of honey-fungus threads in rotting wood, and the glow-worm, a beetle whose wingless female shines from grass banks on summer nights to call the flying males. In the winter of 1907–08 people in Norfolk reported glowing barn owls, perhaps dusted with that fungus.

**The tales.** Travellers follow the light, sure it is a cottage window or a lantern on the causeway, and wake in the bog. Being "pixie-led" breaks if you turn your coat inside out. In the Shropshire tale, Will the Smith is refused by both Heaven and Hell and given a single ember to wander with; in Ireland, Stingy Jack carries his in a turnip. Some lights guard treasure, like the Finnish *aarnivalkea* that burns over buried hoards. In Lincolnshire, Tiddy Mun of the carrs takes his revenge on those who drain the fens.

**Society.** The wisps run the Honourable Company of Fen Guides, a guiding service that parodies the tourist board, the rambling club and a confident satnav. They advertise shortcuts, scenic routes and "the quickest way home", sell maps with paths drawn in fresh ink, and swear every route is very nearly there. They mean well, or say so. Their worry is the Dyke Board, whose drainage dries out their home and puts out lights.

**Notable figures.**
- **Old Will**, the founder, who still carries the smith's ember in a wisp of straw and still won't say where he's going.
- **Captain Spunkie**, head of the Shortcut Office, who has never arrived anywhere.
- **Mademoiselle Follette**, a visiting cousin who leads dances in rings across the water meadows.
- **Glim**, a glow-worm signalwoman who keeps the Company's lamps and the gossip.
- **Little Phos**, an apprentice who can only manage a flicker and is always pointing the wrong way.
- **Gaffer Turncoat**, a toad ferryman who wears his coat inside out and is the only one in the fen never led astray.

**Places.** Lantern Carr, the Company's headquarters on stilts; the Shortcut, a path everyone knows and nobody can find twice; the Sunken Causeway; Will's Forge, a cold hearth on an island of bog-oak; the Seventh Stile, beyond which nobody has walked back; Hoard Mere, where the lights stand over buried things.

**Objects and icons.** Horn lanterns, twists of burning straw, a single ember in a turnip, signposts that face the other way by morning, inked maps, brass "This Way" badges, stepping stones that aren't there, coats worn inside out, reflections of lights in black water.

**Themes.** Misdirection, help that leads you wrong, the light always just ahead, false fire and cold fire, looking at the lantern instead of the ground, breaking the spell, treasure under the bog, and a home that drainage is shrinking.

**The play it suggests:** marsh lights that lead the enemy astray, where the play is deciding what the enemy hits and making it hit the wrong thing.
