## Part A: the seed — The Lekking Knights

**Who they are.** A dwindling chivalric order of capercaillie, the largest grouse in the world, who hold the old fir ridges above Beechhold: cathedral-grey beech trunks below, dark firs in mist above, charcoal smoke in the clearings and tarns where water-nymphs live. Enormous and vain, they duel at dawn as their grandfathers did. Each year there are fewer of them, and each year they refuse to change anything. A cousin chapter keeps the same rites in the spruce forests of Sprucehold, further north.

**The real bird.** A capercaillie cock is a dark slate-grey giant with a glossy green breast, a white spot at the shoulder, a pale hooked bill, a shaggy throat "beard" and a bare scarlet patch over each eye; he weighs about twice what the hen does. The hen is smaller, barred in brown and buff, and vanishes into the heather. In spring the cocks gather before sunrise at a traditional display ground, the lek, used for generations. They fan their round tails, raise their beards, point their bills at the sky and sing a strange quiet song: dry clicks that speed up, a pop like a cork, then a grinding "whetting." During the whetting, hunters long said, the bird is deaf, and they crept closer in those seconds. Rivals fight, sometimes to the death, and a few top cocks get most of the matings; young males display at the edges. Hens choose, then nest on the ground and raise the chicks alone. In winter the birds live on pine needles, swallow grit to grind them, and grow comb-like fringes on their toes that work as snowshoes. Some "mad" cocks display at and attack people, dogs and even cars. The name comes from Gaelic for "horse of the woods." The bird died out in Scotland in the 1700s and was brought back from Sweden; it is protected, and declining, across much of Europe.

**Society and parody.** A knightly order out of medieval romance, with a touch of Don Quixote. The lek is the tourney ground, and each knight holds his station on it by right of the last dawn he won. Elders hold the centre; squires strut at the edges. The hens are not members and are not impressed: they watch from the bilberry, judge, and leave. A knight seeing a rival, even mid-battle, must stop and display. The order guards the old forest by standing in it, and takes new fences, roads and felling as insults.

**Notable figures.** **Grand Master Ottokar**, oldest cock on the oldest lek, who has not lost a dawn in living memory. **Sir Hartmut the Whetting**, so lost in his song that he has never once heard a retreat called. **Sir Brennbart**, a mad cock who challenges woodcutters' carts and the boar raftsmen's wagons. **Squire Konrad**, a yearling on the edge of the list, practising his pop. **Dame Reisig**, the grey hen whose choice the whole lek waits on. **The Chronicler**, who keeps the order's roll and crosses out a name each spring.

**Places.** The Old Lek, a moss clearing on a fir ridge; the Roost Pines where knights sleep aloft; the Bilberry Gallery; the Grit Pits; the Needle Refectory, all winter austerity; the Fence Line, which no knight acknowledges.

**Objects and icons.** The fanned tail as shield and heraldry; the scarlet brow as a mark of rank; the white shoulder badge of the order; the bristling beard; gizzard stones kept as relics; snowshoe toes; the cork-pop; pennons on fir branches; a trophy tail on a hunting-lodge wall as a memento mori.

**Themes.** Vanity and display, honour that won't bend, the single champion, dawn duels, being judged by those who watch, tradition in a shrinking world, deafness to reason, splendid decline.

**The play it suggests:** Few, vain, enormous knights at their best with the stage to themselves: one champion instead of a crowd.
