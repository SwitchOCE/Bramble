## Part A: the seed — The Gale Court

**Who they are.** The Gale Court is the woodland's weather, in person: a royal household of wind and cloud spirits that holds court on the high ridgeways, chalk downs and bare hilltops above the trees, where nothing breaks the wind. They are not animal folk but airy shapes in robes of cloud, attended by the birds that live in the wind. Everyone below talks about them and has no say.

**Real weather and real lore.** The court is built from Europe's real named winds. The **mistral** is a cold, dry northwesterly that roars down the Rhône valley, scrubs the sky clear and is blamed for driving people mad. The **bora** hits the Adriatic in violent cold gusts; in Trieste, ropes were strung along the steepest streets for people to cling to. The **föhn** is a warm, dry wind off the Alps, blamed for headaches and foul tempers. The **Helm Wind** of Cross Fell in the Pennines is often called Britain's only named wind, and wears a cap of cloud, the helm. A high-pressure system is heavy and settled and can sit unmoving for weeks. A thunderhead (cumulonimbus) towers into an anvil, and its thunder always comes after the lightning,; you count the gap to tell how far off it is. Weather lore runs alongside: red sky at night, shepherd's delight; mackerel sky and mares' tails; pine cones closing in damp air. Swifts eat, sleep and mate on the wing and fly around storm fronts; the kestrel, the "windhover", holds still in the air by facing into the wind.

**Society.** A parody of a royal court obsessed with precedence: who enters first, who is announced, who stands where. Winds are named for where they come from, and the court seats them on the old **wind rose**, each in its place on the compass. A herald reads the day's forecast aloud in the same fixed order every time, like the Shipping Forecast, and nobody may interrupt. Courtiers squabble about rank, and the weathercock changes sides with every arrival ("weathercock" really is an old word for a fickle person).

**Notable figures.** **Her Serene Stillness, Lady High Pressure**, the sovereign, who sits for weeks and forbids anything to happen. **Lord Mistral**, Warden of the Rhône, who clears the room. **Countess Bora**, whose arrivals knock courtiers flat. **Old Föhn**, warm, charming and blamed for everything. **The Helm**, who never leaves his one fell and mentions his name to everyone. **The Gust Pages**, a pack of quarrelling squalls who all want to go first. **Duke Cumulonimbus, the Thunderhead**, who arrives late and loud. **Sir Vane**, the weathercock herald on the spire. **Mr and Mrs Fair-and-Foul** of the weather house, never home together. **The Swift Couriers**, who never land. **The Master of the Wild Hunt**, the court's huntsman, who rides the winter storm.

**Places.** The Long Ridgeway; the court's eight-sided **Tower of the Winds**, after the real one in Athens with a wind god carved on each face; beacon hills and old hill forts; dew ponds on the chalk; the church spire where Sir Vane keeps watch.

**Objects and icons.** Weathercocks and wind roses, barometers marked Fair, Change and Stormy, weather houses, Aeolian harps, kites, thistledown, hayricks lifted by whirlwinds (in Irish lore a whirlwind on a still day is the fairy host passing, the *sí gaoithe*), the bag of winds Aeolus gave Odysseus, the anvil cloud. Luke Howard's Latin cloud names (cirrus, cumulus, stratus, nimbus) serve as the court's heraldry.

**Themes.** Precedence and protocol, timing and late entrances, the calm before the storm, sudden changes of wind, fickle loyalty, forecasts read with confidence and often wrong, and a whole land that has to put up with them.

**The play it suggests:** weather that moves in squalls, bending who strikes first, how often and in what order (a suggestion only).
