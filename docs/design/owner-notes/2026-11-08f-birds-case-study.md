# A case study on Birds: find the process failure (8 Nov 2026)

The owner's words to the ideation orchestrator, after reading the playtest round 5 findings on build 7 and the Bird card list.

## Process failures matter more than content failures

Okay, so let's do a case study just on Birds, right? Because I think if we can get a case study, we can reveal process failures. And I think process failures are more important than specific content failures. So let's have a look at Birds.

## Fly South makes Birds unreliable, so they need a reason to be played

The first thing I notice is that the Fly South mechanic, if I understand it correctly, is that Birds basically are migratory. They come and go, right? And they're only half as powerful essentially while they're gone, because you have the Egg, right? So essentially you've always got a Bird at half. Your Birds are unreliable, right? They oscillate between a higher power level and a lower power level because of the flying mechanic. You want something to justify the fact that you've got this unreliable amount of power.

## The early Birds give no reason to buy them

So we've got these enablers on Tavern 1, right? The Chiffchaff *[transcribed as "Cliff Half"]* and One Swallow, which get you some stuff to get going to begin with, right? The idea is you can play the Chiffchaff, a Tavern 1 unit, and when it's played it buffs units in front of it. Realistically, Tavern 1 units are only going to be good on turn 1. So our Tavern 1 units are going to be at their most attractive on turn 1, and then they're going to get out-competed, and they're going to be subpar options until they're poor options.

So when we look at the Chiffchaff, it buffs units in front of it, but on turn 1 you can only buy one unit, so its permanent buff immediately gets wasted, and you're essentially buying a 1/2, right? Its when-played effect *[I'll use the word battlecry or enter the battlefield, whatever you want to call it]* basically doesn't give any value on the turn where it's most important for it to give value. So you wouldn't want to buy it on turn 1. That already hurts the tribe, because it's much less likely you'll have it flying south on the board by the time something like a Mr Redbreast comes along.

And Mr Redbreast doesn't have any particular reason you'd want to purchase it on Tavern 2. It's not well statted. It's just a 1/2 on Tavern 2, and there are better-statted units on Tavern 2. So there's no reason you'd ever want to buy it, really, if you don't already have Nests, which you're only going to have half the time as well.

So just from a power budgeting perspective, you can look at this early game and go: why would I ever want these units on the board in the early game? There's no real justification for picking this over something else. I can't see why you would play the early-game Birds over other cards in the game. Does that make sense?

## A late payoff needs a journey that gets you there

So it doesn't matter how good the other synergies are at higher levels. Obviously, with the Gabble Ratchet, the idea is that you get both halves of the Bird, the good half and the less powerful half, so you don't have to suffer the downsides anymore. Taking away the downsides is the big thematic payoff of the Gabble Ratchet at the end, and that's cool. But you have to have a board that gets you there in the first place. You have to have some synergy that gets you there along the entire journey. And when you look at Birds, there's no real strategic reason. Even if you were offered a bunch of these units together at once, the strategic payoff doesn't feel like it would be better than going into another tribe. So there's been a failure of power budgeting, and also of game-plan unfolding, if you will: the gameplay journey.

## Find out how it got through the process

And what you need to understand is how that got through your process. The important part is not necessarily all these issues, which can individually be fixed. And maybe my take is off: this is a first assessment, and I'm just taking a look at these as an experienced game designer and going, this is what I suspect is going on. I could be off on this, but I suspect this is probably an accurate assessment of the tribe. You should verify it, because this is my hypothesis.

So if this is the case, you need to find out how this slipped through your process. Because I didn't even have to spend 10 seconds as a human, and that's a short period of time for a human, not an agent, to see the issues here. It's a fundamental misunderstanding of how these games are played, I guess.

## How these games are played: early cards shape later choices

You want to pick up cards that are strong early, help you out and give you some sort of advantage in the early game. Those cards then inform your future decisions, because they change how likely you are to want other effects, right? If there are a bunch of really good early-game Birds that use Fly South, a card like Mr Redbreast suddenly looks way more attractive. But if there aren't any good early-game Birds, a card like Mr Redbreast looks like a terrible card, because the cards it synergises with aren't cards you would want to buy individually. That's probably how you need to solve these issues: by looking at these things more as entire strategic pieces.

## Even card counts and tier spreads across tribes

When you're designing Birds, and you've given me this list of all the Birds in front of me, I'm immediately noticing that we should have an even number of cards for every tribe, and an even tier distribution for every tribe as well, I would say, right? That's actually one of the problems Battlegrounds has: it doesn't do this. If 22 Quilboar exist and only 17 Undead, the player is just more likely to find good pieces to make Quilboar work. Even if the Quilboar cards are individually weaker, the Quilboar tribe ends up stronger, and you'll make stronger Quilboar boards more often, because there are more cards and more likely more powerful ones. So we want an even distribution, so that tribes have fair effect competition, because all effects in game design compete with other effects.

That's my hypothesis of what's probably going wrong. There are some principles I apply that help me get to these conclusions quickly, and I think this is likely to be useful feedback for you. Again, I could be wrong, but check it out.

## Triage the issue before fixing it (8 Nov, the owner directly to the orchestrator, a few minutes later)

I think this is a real part of my decision-making process when it comes to games and balance and metagame health: triaging the issue. When you go to an issue, you want to take a step back and then triage what's causing it, right? You can form multiple hypotheses about which things could be causing the problem you're experiencing, and then look for evidence for each of those. Immediately, the hypothesis I formed was about early-game weakness making it so that the payoffs didn't make sense, and there didn't seem to be enough payoffs. But whether that hypothesis turns out to be true or not, it's more about the process: when you see an issue, I want you to go through this kind of triage process. Maybe even write yourself a little skill. I'll approve it if you want.

And when looking at a piece of content, ask: when is the player going to want this? On what turns of the game? At what stages of the game is this content intended to be attractive to the player? Implicit in that is the understanding that there are some situations, and many board states, where the player will not want to buy that card. It will not be a good use of resources. And make sure that each piece of content, designed for a purpose and a role to enable a wider strategy, actually fulfils that purpose. If it's not fulfilling its primary purpose, then it's always useless and it becomes a trap.

## Journeys: look for skips, and don't make tribes formulaic (8 Nov, the owner directly to the orchestrator, after approving the triage skill)

One thing I'd note is you're maybe a little simplistic in the skill on the journey thing. It's fine for the skill itself, but a lot of problems actually come from skips in the journey. In Battlegrounds there are a lot of units that people just skip over. They play tempo till tier 4, then skip tier 5 entirely and go straight to the big powerful tier 6 units. Or they don't use any of the tier 4 pieces and skip straight to tier 5. That's because the journey is not fundamentally well distributed in terms of power, and there are individual pieces that are in themselves traps and not worth the payoff. And with the journey thing, you don't want to be overly prescriptive either, because we still want there to be discovery rather than planning a tribe to be formulaic, right? We want there to be multiple different sub-varieties of options you can do with the tribe that might be synergistic.
